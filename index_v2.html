Research

Two questions, one lens

Outside the firm

From Regulation to Markets

How do rules become working markets, and why do some fail between policy intent and real outcomes?

  1. Rules & recognition
  2. Implementation & strategic response
  3. Markets & social outcomes

A rule creates categories, eligibility, and priority. It does not implement itself. Someone has to build and finance the systems that make recognition usable, participants adapt to the rule or game it, and the market that results can select very different actors than the rule-makers intended. My earlier work followed this in health care, where competition pushed transplant centers to misreport a measured allocation criterion, and where nonprofit, for-profit, and public hospitals each bent the same rule toward the people their form exists to serve.

Now working onEnvironmental certification and transition finance: who ends up inside rules written on verifiable attributes like forest cover and water quality, and who builds, finances, and governs the verification. Settings include aquaculture certification in the Mekong Delta, commodity certification in Southeast Asia, and coastal quality programs in Europe, all measured from public satellite and administrative data.

Inside the firm

From Knowledge to Action

How do organizations turn representations, records, culture, and shared knowledge into coordinated capability?

  1. Knowledge structures
  2. Judgment & coordination
  3. Capability & performance

Organizations decide what people pay attention to, how performance gets recorded, what knowledge is shared, and what becomes common enough to act on together. Those choices drive judgment, coordination, and learning. They also decide how much value a firm gets from artificial intelligence, because a shared AI tool changes not only how much people know but how alike they think.

Now working onCulture as a coordination device: why a cultural faux pas costs so much (it marks someone who will misread the next game), and how organizations design what is known about people, by members, and in common.

The lens across both

Uncertainty is shaped, not just faced. Rules decide what can be measured and rewarded. Representations decide which possibilities make it into a decision. Competitive position changes what variance is worth. Organizations decide which uncertainty gets reduced, shared, preserved, or exploited.

  • Working paperSolo-authored

    The Firm as Shared Cognition

    A shared AI tool has two properties: how smart it is and how common it makes thinking across the firm. Commonness helps teams coordinate but makes the firm fragile when the shared tool is wrong, and it changes whether employees will teach the tool what they know.

    Competing Representations

    A firm's way of seeing the market is only as good as the rival it faces. Performance depends on what you attend to and also on what your competitor sees and misses.

About & CV

Engineer, economist, strategist

Jim Ostler is Associate Dean of Graduate Programs and Associate Professor and JELD-WEN Chair of Free Enterprise at Willamette University's Atkinson School of Business. He builds simple formal models of strategy and organization and tests them where the data are unusually clean: allocation rules in health care, certification schemes visible from satellites, a decade of student stock picks.

Before Willamette he taught at the Wharton School, Georgetown's McDonough School of Business, and the University of Michigan's Ross School of Business. He trained first as a chemical engineer, which may explain the fondness for models you can actually run.
